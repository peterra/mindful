# Eye Position & Focus in Meditation

## The three canonical approaches

**1. Closed eyes** — Most common in modern/Western mindfulness. Reduces visual input, makes internal visualization (chakras, breath, body scan) easier, and deepens relaxation. The classic downside: drowsiness. Teachers across traditions note that closed-eye practice frequently collapses into a semi-sleep state, especially for beginners.

**2. Half-lidded ("soft gaze")** — The dominant posture in Zen (zazen), Tibetan Buddhism, and much of the Theravada tradition. Eyes are ~40% open, unfocused, gaze resting 2–4 feet ahead or on the floor. Called "lion's gaze" in some Buddhist texts. It keeps the practitioner alert without giving the visual cortex strong input to lock onto — a middle path between withdrawal and engagement.

**3. Open eyes** — Used in Bodhidharma's Zen wall-gazing, Dzogchen sky-gazing, and Trataka (yogic candle-gazing). The argument: meditation should train presence *in* the world, not retreat from it. Open-eye practice transfers more directly into everyday awareness.

## Why eye position matters — the mechanisms

- **Arousal regulation.** The oculomotor system is tightly coupled to the reticular activating system. Closed eyes drop arousal (alpha rises, drowsiness follows); a soft downward gaze holds an alert-but-calm state. This is likely why every major contemplative tradition that runs *long* sessions (Zen, Tibetan) uses open or half-open eyes — closed eyes don't scale past ~20 minutes without sleep pressure.
- **Attention anchoring.** Spontaneous micro-saccades and eye drift correlate with mind-wandering. A 2019 PLOS One study found eye-movement amplitude in the delta band is significantly smaller during focused-attention meditation than during instructed mind-wandering — i.e., a *still* gaze is a measurable marker of a still mind. Fixing the eyes fixes attention, and vice versa.
- **Default Mode Network suppression.** Closing the eyes increases DMN activity (self-referential thought, rumination). Open or soft-gaze practice appears to attenuate this, which is one reason Zen traditions consider closed-eye meditation a beginner's crutch.
- **State specificity of research.** A large majority of meditation neuroscience studies (43 of ~52 in recent reviews) used eyes-closed protocols, which means much of what we "know" about meditation's EEG signature is confounded with the eyes-closed alpha rebound. Eyes-open studies show that state-mindfulness scores are *higher* in eyes-closed conditions but attention drifts *less* in eyes-open conditions — a real trade-off, not a settled question.

## Trataka — the explicit "eye practice"

Trataka (Sanskrit: "to gaze") is the only major technique where the eyes are the *object* of meditation. Practitioner fixes a soft gaze on a candle flame (or dot, symbol, deity image) until the eyes water, then closes them and holds the afterimage internally. Traditionally classified as a *shatkarma* (cleansing practice) rather than meditation proper. Claimed benefits span ocular health, concentration, insomnia, and pineal/"third-eye" activation. Modern studies (small samples, mostly Indian journals) report improvements in selective attention, working memory, and sleep quality — suggestive but not definitive.

## Practical synthesis

The common thread across traditions: **eye position is a lever for arousal, not a matter of doctrine.** Sleepy? Open them more. Overstimulated? Soften or close them. The gaze should be *placed*, not *strained* — a held, unfocused rest somewhere below the horizontal, not a staring contest. Stillness of the eyes is both an input to and a readout of attentional stability.
